EU’s digital omnibus packages introduce major regulatory shifts to boost innovation

By FTS Briefing Newsroom · 88 days ago

European Union unveils comprehensive reform to streamline digital laws Delays high-risk AI system obligations to favour industry adaptation Moves aim to balance innovation growth with privacy protections, amid criticism

On 19 November 2025, the European Union unveiled two "Omnibus" reform packages aimed at regulatory simplification to boost innovation and growth within its digital economy. These packages, introduced by the European Commission, target several critical regulatory domains, including artificial intelligence (AI), data protection, cybersecurity, and data governance, with the goal of streamlining existing rules that many businesses find burdensome.

Central to this initiative is the "Digital Omnibus" package, which proposes amendments to key legislation such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the ePrivacy Directive, the Network and Information Security Directive (NIS2), and the Data Act. A separate component, the "Digital Omnibus on AI," suggests changes to the EU AI Act. Together, these reforms aim to reduce administrative overhead and promote a more innovation-friendly environment across the EU.

One of the most notable changes involves narrowing the GDPR's definition of personal data. The commission proposes excluding data from the definition if the holder of that data lacks the "means reasonably likely to be used" to identify an individual. This marks a significant adjustment to GDPR’s core concepts, aligning them with recent case law, such as the Court of Justice of the European Union's ruling in the SRB case. Furthermore, the proposal tasks the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) with developing unified EU-wide lists of processing activities that require or do not require Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs), replacing the current fragmented national standards. The EDPB would also create standardised DPIA templates and methodologies for regular updating, ensuring they remain relevant with emerging technologies.

The proposal also broadens exemptions to data subject rights, particularly when personal data is deemed low risk or processed for scientific research. It introduces clearer conditions under which data controllers may refuse access requests or levy fees to prevent abuses of GDPR rights that do not genuinely aim at protecting personal data. A major development here is the explicit recognition of AI development as a "legitimate interest" under GDPR’s Article 6, allowing organisations to process personal data for AI training and operation while still requiring strict necessity, proportionality, and appropriate safeguards, including data minimisation and the data subject’s unconditional right to object. Significantly, this also suggests that special category data may be used lawfully under certain conditions, signaling a potential softening in privacy protections around sensitive data used in AI.

Complementing these changes, the Omnibus sets out new derogations under Article 9 of GDPR permitting the processing of special category data for AI, so long as sufficient measures are taken to prevent unlawful disclosure, or where biometric data processing is controlled solely by the user, such as on-device fingerprint recognition.

The ePrivacy Directive is modernised as well, aiming to improve user experience online by reducing the frequency of cookie banners and giving users centralised control over cookie preferences through browser or operating system settings. Consent must be easy to give or refuse with simple mechanisms like a single click, and if consent is refused, cookie requests cannot be repeated for the same purpose for at least six months.

The Omnibus also seeks to consolidate cybersecurity and data breach reporting frameworks by introducing a single-entry point for companies to meet all incident-reporting requirements under various laws, including NIS2, GDPR, and the Digital Operational Resilience Act. In cases where a personal data breach poses a high risk to individuals’ rights, the controller must notify through this single channel without undue delay, ideally within 96 hours.

Key provisions under the Data Act include stronger safeguards for trade secrets, granting data holders the right to refuse sharing certain trade-secret-protected information if there is a significant risk of unlawful access or misuse, especially involving jurisdictions with insufficient protections. Targeted exemptions from cloud-switching obligations have been introduced, benefiting bespoke and highly customised data processing services and small to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) or small mid-caps by allowing continuation of early-termination fees in fixed-term contracts. The proposal also removes the controversial earlier requirement for smart contract compliance in data-sharing agreements, reducing complexity for industries reliant on automated data exchange.

Most prominently newsworthy is the proposed delay to the enforcement of high-risk AI system obligations under the AI Act. Instead of taking effect in August 2026, compliance deadlines have been extended until either six months after the Commission sets technical requirements for remote biometric identification systems listed in Annex III, or 12 months after technical rule-setting for AI systems listed under Annex I, such as medical devices and toys. At the latest, obligations will come into force by December 2027 for AI used in law enforcement and education, and August 2028 for other applications. This delay followed considerable lobbying from Big Tech companies, who argued for more time to adapt to the regulatory demands. It has been viewed as a balance between reducing bureaucratic burdens and preserving robust regulatory standards.

The Commission’s AI Office would be granted broader supervisory and enforcement powers under the revised AI Act, including exclusive authority over general-purpose AI models and AI embedded in very large online platforms and search engines. This office would hold capabilities such as requesting documentation, overseeing pre-market conformity assessments, and imposing penalties, thus creating a centralised point of oversight for major AI developers in the EU. Interestingly, mandatory AI literacy requirements for companies have been replaced with encouragement for training and best practice sharing, reflecting a softer approach to promoting AI understanding in businesses.

The Commission claims these measures will allow European businesses to focus more on innovation and competitiveness. It estimates administrative cost savings up to €5 billion by 2029 due to regulatory simplification. However, the proposals have faced criticism that they might weaken two decades of privacy protections, especially by easing access to sensitive personal data like health and biometric information for AI training without explicit consent. Critics fear these changes may prioritise competitiveness and industry convenience over fundamental data rights.

Moving forward, the Omnibus packages will be subject to review, amendment, and adoption by the European Parliament and the Council, representing member states. Political divisions remain, particularly between proponents of strict data protection and those advocating for greater digital market competitiveness, possibly causing delays. The AI Act amendments are especially time-sensitive, needing to be finalised before the original August 2026 deadline when most AI Act provisions become applicable. Following adoption, the amendments will take effect almost immediately, although the final text may differ from the initial proposals as a result of parliamentary negotiations.

In summary, the EU’s Digital Omnibus reform represents a substantial effort to recalibrate regulatory frameworks in the digital space. It aims to temper regulatory complexity while enhancing European businesses’ innovation potential, but not without raising important debates on the balance between data protection and competitive flexibility within the fast-evolving tech landscape.
