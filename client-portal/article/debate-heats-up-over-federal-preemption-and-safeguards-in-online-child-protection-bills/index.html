Debate heats up over federal preemption and safeguards in online child protection bills

By FTS Briefing Newsroom · 88 days ago

House subcommittee reviews nearly twenty measures to shield children online Concerns raised over broad federal preemption overriding state protections Experts highlight risks of biometric data, AI regulation, and speech restrictions

The House Energy and Commerce Subcommittee on Commerce, Manufacturing and Trade spent a session this week weighing nearly two dozen proposals aimed at strengthening protections for children and teens online, but witnesses warned that sweeping federal preemption clauses in several bills could strip away stronger safeguards that states currently provide. According to the original report, the hearing examined 19 measures spanning age verification, data privacy, parental controls, artificial intelligence, screen time and digital literacy, with particular focus on updates to the Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA) and a revamp of the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act, often dubbed COPPA 2.0. [1][2][7]

The centerpiece of the debate was KOSA’s proposed “duty of care” for platforms, which would require companies to prevent and mitigate harms such as cyberbullying, addictive product design and content promoting self-harm or exploitation. Proponents argue the bill would mandate defaults that limit harmful interactions, allow users to disable addictive features and provide opt-outs from algorithmic recommendations. Committee leadership described the effort as an attempt to set a national standard and give regulators tools to hold platforms accountable. [1][6][2]

Critics at the hearing, however, urged clearer definitions and safeguards to prevent unintended consequences. “I see a number of gaps that would be left by the legislation, the biggest one that I see is the preemption standard that is within all of the bills,” Kate Ruane, director of the Free Expression Project at the Center for Democracy and Technology, told the committee. She warned: “If we finish this process with the standard in place, we will likely wind up with children having less protections at the state level than they do today, and we will have failed to do our jobs.” Ruane and others urged Congress either to narrow preemption or to allow states to layer stronger protections on top of a federal floor. [1][3]

Privacy proposals under COPPA 2.0 would extend protections for children beyond the current under‑13 threshold, sometimes up to age 16, ban targeted advertising to teens without consent and expand rights to access, correct and delete personal information. Yet witnesses cautioned that age-assurance mechanisms needed careful design: they can require additional collection or processing of sensitive data and raise risks of breaches, chilling effects on private engagement, and misuses of biometric or identity information. These concerns were echoed across expert testimony and congressional discussion. [1][5][4]

Civil liberties advocates emphasised the tension between safety and access to information, warning that overly broad or vague rules could incentivise platforms to remove lawful but valuable speech. “We are still concerned that it will scare companies into removing lawful speech that could be valuable or even lifesaving for teens,” Jenna Leventoff, senior policy counsel at the American Civil Liberties Union, said in written comments. She cautioned that poorly scoped language might silence content ranging from jokes and hyperbole to vital sex‑education and suicide‑prevention resources. [1]

A related flashpoint was the prospect of federal preemption of state AI laws. Witnesses flagged the risk that an AI moratorium attached to kids‑safety legislation could halt state innovation and oversight at a time when states are actively investigating how chatbots and other generative systems affect young people. “It’s not clear to me that the AI moratorium will become attached to some kids safety package, but if it was, it would be tremendously ill advised,” Ruane said, noting states’ ongoing work to assess AI’s privacy and safety impacts. State technology officials have urged Congress to reject broad preemption of state AI rules even as it crafts federal protections. [1][3][6]

Not all testimony aligned: child protection organisations, citing trends in exploitation and new threats enabled by generative AI, urged robust platform responsibilities and more effective age assurance shared among platforms, app stores and parental tools. The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children has supported several proposed bills addressing online harms and urged that platforms be held to higher standards for detecting and removing abusive material. The committee’s record shows a spectrum of views about how to balance enforcement, technical feasibility and civil‑liberties safeguards. [4][5][2]

As the Subcommittee proceeds, the central challenge will be reconciling a desire for uniform national protections with the policy diversity and experimentation occurring at state level, while ensuring legislative language is precise enough to avoid chilling speech or creating privacy harms through well‑intentioned compliance regimes. Industry, civil‑liberties groups and child‑safety organisations all call for clearer definitions, of “duty of care,” of permissible data practices and of the boundaries of federal preemption, if Congress is to deliver durable protections for young people online. [1][2][3][5]
