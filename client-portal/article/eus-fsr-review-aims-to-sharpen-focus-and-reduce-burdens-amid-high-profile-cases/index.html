EU’s FSR review aims to sharpen focus and reduce burdens amid high-profile cases

By FTS Briefing Newsroom · 88 days ago

The European Commission reviews the Foreign Subsidies Regulation to improve effectiveness and streamline procedures. Legal experts recommend revising thresholds and simplifying reporting to target significant distortions. Ongoing high-profile cases like ADNOC’s €14.7 billion deal highlight enforcement challenges and policy tensions.

In August 2025, the European Commission initiated a comprehensive review of the Foreign Subsidies Regulation (FSR), a framework designed to address the distortive effects of foreign state subsidies within the EU market. This review, covering the regulation’s implementation and enforcement over its first two years, is set to conclude with a formal report and potentially accompanying legislative proposals by mid-2026. The review aims to refine and simplify the FSR to enhance its effectiveness while reducing undue burdens on businesses and enforcement authorities alike.

A detailed submission by Cleary Gottlieb, a prominent law firm, outlines key recommendations to recalibrate the FSR. Central to their proposal is revising the jurisdictional thresholds that currently trigger mandatory notifications for mergers and public tenders, which have resulted in a high volume of filings but very few in-depth interventions, less than 1%. The firm advocates for raising or reframing these financial thresholds, aligning reporting obligations more closely with the actual scope of foreign financial contributions, and individualizing thresholds to apply to single parties rather than aggregated amounts. Such changes aim to focus regulatory scrutiny on transactions that truly risk distorting market competition, thereby conserving Commission resources and minimizing business disruption.

Additionally, Cleary Gottlieb suggests streamlining the notification and review procedures to shorten timelines and simplify reporting. Proposed measures include introducing simplified forms for straightforward cases, allowing the Commission flexibility to issue early decisions within the initial 25-working-day review phase, and enhancing transparency and predictability through clearer information requests and better communication with notifying parties. Improvements specific to public tenders are also recommended, such as enabling direct submission of filings to the Commission, bypassing contracting authorities that may impose inconsistent processes, and developing a public register to track case progress and outcomes.

The firm further highlights the disproportionate impact of the FSR on private investment funds, which constitute about 30% of merger filings but tend not to raise subsidy concerns. They urge the Commission to broaden eligibility for the private fund disclosure exemption and introduce a fast-track review process for straightforward fund investments, particularly where investments are passive and limited to partnerships.

Substantively, the review addresses the need for clearer guidance on how the Commission assesses the distortion caused by foreign subsidies and the balancing of any positive effects against competition concerns. Cleary Gottlieb calls for defining the nature of harm the FSR aims to prevent, clarifying the concept of “benefit” in intra-group transactions with an arm’s-length principle, and ensuring robust causal links between subsidies and market distortions. They also recommend establishing a safe harbour for certain foreign subsidies aligned with existing EU State aid rules to enhance legal certainty. The firm's input stresses the importance of transparent criteria for remedies and commitments offered under the FSR, encompassing standards for their assessment and parameters for scope, type, and duration.

Parallel to the review process, the European Commission is expected to publish detailed guidelines on the FSR’s distortion and balancing tests by January 2026. This follows a broad public consultation held earlier in 2025, which sought input on the thresholds for intervention and criteria for call-in powers concerning below-threshold mergers and tenders.

The practical implications of the FSR and its enforcement are already visible in high-profile cases. For example, the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC)’s proposed €14.7 billion acquisition of German chemical firm Covestro has come under investigation. The Commission is examining whether UAE-state subsidies linked to this deal, such as an unlimited guarantee and a capital injection, distort competition within the EU market. ADNOC has criticised the EU’s demands for information as disproportionate and intrusive, warning that they may jeopardise the transaction. The Commission paused its investigation to await ADNOC’s submission of requested documents, with a decision initially expected by December 2, 2025. This case underscores the challenges faced by large non-EU investors navigating the FSR regime and highlights ongoing tensions between state-backed acquisitions and EU competition policy.

The FSR review and accompanying refinements come at a time when the EU is balancing the need for robust market protections with broader economic and regulatory simplification efforts. For instance, recent reforms to the EU’s Common Agricultural Policy have sought to relax environmental standards to alleviate regulatory burdens on farmers, illustrating a wider trend towards easing administrative pressures on businesses while maintaining core objectives.

As the Commission prepares to publish its report and potentially propose amendments, stakeholders across industries will be watching closely to see how the FSR evolves to balance competitiveness, transparency, and fairness in an increasingly globalised market environment.
